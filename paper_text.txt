ABSTRACT Emotion Recognition is a critical area of research including healthcare, human-computer
interaction, and psychology. While traditional methods mainly rely on facial expressions and textual analysis,
they also have inherent flaws and cannot be reliable. Facial expression-based emotion recognition assumes
that it represents genuine internal emotions that may be inaccurate. Similarly, textual analysis depends on the
available data and needs help accurately capturing subtle emotions in text. However, electroencephalography
(EEG) has emerged as a rising alternative for objective and real-time emotion recognition. Unlike facial
and textual methods, EEG directly measures brain activity and provides a reliable result. To address this
researchers have used basic machine learning methods that need manual feature extraction, which might
miss essential data and make the process slow and less accurate. In this study, we propose a comprehensive
methodology for EEG-based emotion recognition that addresses the limitations of traditional methods and
basic machine learning techniques. Our approach involves preprocessing EEG signals using a butter-worth
bandpass filter to eliminate noise, followed by feature extraction techniques. We then employ Principal
Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction, ensuring efficient data representation. To further
enhance the model performance we explore machine learning classifiers(GaussianNB, SVM, Random
Forest) and proposed an EEG-LSTM and GRU model with an accuracy of 97% and 96% respectively, that
gives better results than the basic machine learning models.

II. RELATED WORKS

Zhen et al. [10] in their study discussed the importance of
face recognition and the existing literature on the neural
mechanisms involved. It was necessary to do research on
human participants utilizing fMRI, which recorded neural
activity as the subjects performed facial recognition tasks.
The authors analyzed the data using network analysis
techniques to identify the hierarchical organization of brain
regions involved in face recognition. The results showed
that face recognition involved a hierarchical network of
brain regions, with lower-level regions responsible for basic
visual processing and higher-level regions responsible for
more complex cognitive processes such as facial identity
recognition. They discussed the importance of a hierarchical
organization in the brain network for efficient face recognition. The findings offered implications for understanding
the neural mechanisms of face recognition and developing
more effective techniques for artificial face recognition.
It did not investigate the effects of other elements, such as
mood or context, on the neural network responsible for face
recognition in the brain.
Pandey and Seeja [11] in their study aimed to recognize
emotional states using electroencephalogram (EEG) signals
through a subject-independent approach. They discussed
the importance of emotion recognition in various fields
and the challenges associated with traditional methods of
emotion recognition. It involved collecting EEG signals from
human subjects while they were exposed to emotionally
evocative stimuli. They were able to classify the different
emotional states by using machine learning models after
training them with strategies for extracting characteristics
from the EEG signals. The approach that was suggested was
successful to the extent of 86.4%, in identifying six different
emotional states, namely happy, sorrow, anger, fear, disgust,
and surprise. This study used a limited number of emotional
states, and further research may be necessary to extend the
method to more complex emotional states.
Doma and Pirouz [12] explored how EEG and peripheral
physiological inputs from human participants are employed
in emotion recognition with ML algorithms when they were
exposed to emotionally expressive stimuli. They extracted
key elements from the signals using feature extraction and
trained machine learning models to classify the emotional
states. They employed the random forest technique, which
outperforms other machine learning approaches in emotion
recognition when using both EEG and periphery physiological inputs.
Tan et al. [13] in their study proposed a multimodal
emotion detection system that improved the accuracy of emotion recognition carried out by integrating facial expression
analysis and EEG. This study involved 20 participants who
were shown emotional images while recording their facial
expressions and EEG signals. They analyzed the EEG signals
using a wavelet transform, then they utilized a CNN to extract
information from the facial expressions. They were input into

Hassouneh et al. [8] discussed previous studies on emotion
recognition, including various methods such as physiological
signals, facial expressions, and speech analysis. They also
highlighted the importance of real-time emotion recognition
systems in various fields, such as healthcare and HCI.
They explained the feature extraction and selection process
before training the ML and deep neural network approaches.
The performance evaluation of the developed system using
various metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.
They also reported the high accuracy and effectiveness of the
developed system in recognizing different emotional states.
Gao et al. [14] in their study introduced Deep Learning
(DL) algorithms as a promising approach to overcome
these challenges. Then, they explained the feature extraction
process before training the DL models. The performance
evaluation of the developed system using various metrics
such as accuracy and confusion matrix. They highlighted the
potential of DL algorithms in improving emotion recognition
from EEG signals. They also suggested future research
directions for improving the system's performance, including
the use of more advanced deep learning architectures and the
integration of multiple modalities of data.
In their study, Schyns et al. [15] explored the neural
mechanisms that allowed humans to categorize facial expressions.When participants saw images of faces expressing
various emotions, their brain activity was monitored using
fMRI. They found that the brain processed facial expressions
in a hierarchical manner, with lower-level visual features
(such as eye and mouth movements) being processed
first, followed by higher-level features (such as the overall
expression). The brain uses a dynamic integration process
to categorize facial expressions, with different stages of
processing occurring at different times. They also suggested that this dynamic process may be important for the
perception of complex stimuli in general, not just facial
expressions.
Similarly, Ismail et al. [6] looked over the feasibility of
using EEG to detect human emotions. They conducted an
experiment in which participants were asked to watch short
video clips designed to evoke different emotions while their
brain activity was recorded using EEG. They discovered that
many emotional states, including happiness, sorrow, anger,
and fear, were related to patterns of brain activity in the
parietal and frontal brain regions. EEG has the potential to be

from EEG signals. As illustrated in our workflow, EEG

and an updated hidden state vector ht at each time step t by
taking an input vector xt and a hidden state vector ht−1 as
inputs. The hidden state vector ht contains information about
the context of the input sequence up to time t. It has three
gates - input, forget, and output gates.

classifying EEG brain signals into different emotions. By this
work, the ability to classify emotions with EEG signals could
enable more personalized and adaptive human-computer
interaction and improve our understanding of the neural
mechanisms underlying emotional processing. However,
there are some limitations. The dataset was small and only
included EEG data from people watching emotional videos.
